Spotlight on Alaska

Kenai Peninsula Drafts Districtwide AI Policies

Kenai Peninsula Borough School District board members got their first look in July at a set of districtwide policy proposals on artificial intelligence. The proposals came from an AI committee the district established last year, facilitated by virtual learning coordinator Amanda Adams, which used DEED's state AI framework as its starting point.

Rather than write a standalone AI policy, the committee is folding AI language into existing policy. One change bars staff from feeding student data into AI platforms the district has not approved. Another subjects AI use to the same guidelines that already govern social media. The largest proposed change rewrites academic honesty to define acceptable AI use and set consequences for misuse.

District staff already have access to MagicSchool and Google Gemini plus training on both.

Deepfakes and Synthetic Media ‐ Modernizing Policies

Nationwide polling indicates roughly 15% of students are aware of AI-generated explicit images depicting their classmates. During the 2023-24 school year, 20% of middle schools and 22% of high schools reported bullying incidents involving AI-generated deepfakes. Only 23% of schools have updated their policies to address AI misuse. (Source: RAND Research, 2025)

DEED's guidance covers what to change in acceptable use, anti-bullying, and student conduct policy; how to run a victim-centered incident response; and which Alaska statutes already apply (AS 11.61.120, AS 11.61.123, AS 11.41.455) alongside the federal TAKE IT DOWN Act.

First-Week Norms: Use the Data

Common Sense Media's 2026 census found 86% of kids ages 9-17 use AI, and nearly a quarter use it daily. Three-quarters say their school has told them what they can and cannot use AI for, but only about half have been taught how to use it safely. More than four in ten say no parent or guardian has ever discussed AI safety with them.

Discussion: What does your class already know how to do with AI, and what has anyone actually taught them? Build the first-week norms conversation from the gap between those two answers.

Code.org Is Now CodeAI

Code.org has rebranded as CodeAI, formalizing a shift from coding-first to what it calls digital fluency: computer science, AI science, and data science together. The number driving it — 84% of students use AI, but only 16% of public high school leaders report students are taught how it actually works.

Existing courses, accounts, URLs, and data agreements carry over. Two curricula are new or expanded:

  • AI Foundations is now a full-year high school pathway. Semester 2 covers how AI works, creating with it, and ethical judgment, with an embedded AI teaching assistant for teachers.
  • AI Discoveries extends CS Discoveries for middle school.

Also worth a look for anyone in policy work: CodeAI published 10 Policies for Digital Fluency, a revision of the CS policy framework it has pushed in all 50 states.

Experience AI — Free Lessons for Ages 11-14

Experience AI is a joint program from the Raspberry Pi Foundation and Google DeepMind, and it won the 2025 UNESCO King Hamad Bin Isa Al-Khalifa Prize for the Use of ICT in Education. The lessons come with plans, slide decks, worksheets, and videos, and are written so teachers outside CS can run them.

Two companions worth knowing about: the Experience AI Challenge, an open-ended project where students train their own machine learning model and build an app around it, and Understanding AI for Educators, a free three-week online course.

Day of AI Student Senate ‐ Students Write the AI Policy

Day of AI, MIT RAISE, the Edward M. Kennedy Institute, and AASA built a free curriculum that puts students in the role of U.S. Senators drafting, debating, and voting on an AI policy for their own school. Students research how AI affects their community, gather stakeholder input, and produce a policy they can bring to school leaders.

Two students from every state met in Boston on July 18-19 to debate a national policy on AI use in K-12 schools on the Senate floor at the EMK Institute. The classroom materials are free, self-paced, and would be a great addition to any student government organization.

Watch: NOVA ‐ A.I. Revolution, Plus a Companion Worksheet

The one-hour NOVA documentary A.I. Revolution follows correspondent Miles O'Brien through what researchers were attempting with AI across medicine, climate science, and robotics. A companion worksheet for educators is posted on the Alaska Education Exchange, built to structure a class conversation about AI's impact on different industries.

The film aired in 2024, which is the reason to use it rather than a reason to skip it. Two years is several eras in this field. Watch it with students and have them mark what has already happened, what has not, and what nobody in the film saw coming.

Discussion: Which predictions in this film aged well? Which ones look naive now — and which ones were not ambitious enough?

CyberPatriot 19 ‐ Registration Open

Registration for the 2026-27 CyberPatriot season opened May 1. The National Youth Cyber Defense Competition puts middle and high school teams in charge of securing virtual networks.

  • Training Round: A basic practice round that includes an answer key – September
  • Official Practice Round: Final practice round before the scored rounds begin – October
  • Scored/Live Rounds: Competitions take place on specified weekends throughout the school year. Scored rounds are held one weekend a month from October to January. Teams choose any four-hour period during the competition weekend (Thursday, Friday, Saturday, or Sunday) to complete all their work.

Registration fees run $205 for high school teams and $165 for middle school teams, with waivers available for all-girl teams, Title I schools, and JROTC/CAP/NSCC teams. Team registration typically closes the first week of October.

NASA TechRise Student Challenge ‐ Opens September

The 2026-27 NASA TechRise Student Challenge opens in September and invites teams in grades 6-12 to submit experiment ideas. This year's flight vehicle is the high-altitude balloon, offering roughly 4-8 hours at 70,000-95,000 feet with exposure to high-altitude radiation and Earth's atmosphere.

Sixty winning teams receive $1,500 to build their experiment, a flight slot on a NASA-sponsored vehicle, and technical support from TechRise advisors. The challenge includes free classroom resources, a student virtual field trip in the fall, and information sessions on request. No experience necessary.
